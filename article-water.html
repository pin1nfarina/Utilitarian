Scylla's Data Centers: The Incredible Water Consumption of Data Centers in California, Why It Matters, and How to Manage It

The Utilitarian Foundation Research Team Environmental & Water Policy

California's ability to secure its economic and sustainability interests in the expanding market of data centers depends on holistic legislation surrounding water usage by these corporations. California has significant leverage to build relationships with data centers, on both a local and state level, that increase economic investment without sacrificing sustainable water use or public trust. Data centers currently face growing scrutiny over a lack of transparency in their water usage. A two-pronged approach — mandating statewide reporting while incentivizing co-investment in recycled water infrastructure — can build public confidence and ease continued economic growth, while helping California stay ahead of worsening drought conditions.

Recycled Water Policy

Data center water usage is the central focus of this report. Data centers use water primarily for cooling servers, and the amount used varies widely with cooling technology, size, and efficiency — from roughly 18,000 to 500,000 gallons per day. Regulating this usage matters both for public well-being and for the state's broader sustainability goals. According to the UC Berkeley Center for Law, Energy, and the Environment's report Regulating Data Center Water Usage in California, data centers generally draw from four sources: commercial water services, recycled water, surface water, and groundwater.

Commercial water services are common for any large-scale private water use, and while they aren't specifically regulated for data centers, California regulates public and private water providers as a whole. When a data center's needs exceed what private vendors can supply, it may enter into a relationship with a municipal water provider — and it's important that local authorities manage the resulting cost and resource pressures carefully to avoid disputes over water rights with residents.

Recycled water remains a minor source of supply for data centers today, generally used only when a facility is built near existing infrastructure. Recycled water plants are smaller and rarer nationally, though California has invested heavily in expanding them: as of 2021, 267 facilities were capable of producing Title 22 recycled water suitable for commercial cooling. The state's 2022 Water Supply Strategy targets 800,000 acre-feet of recycled water by 2030 and 1.4 million acre-feet by 2040, making this a fast-growing sector.

Ground and surface water, by contrast, are only lightly regulated in California. Commercial users are not generally required to report withdrawal rates, and oversight happens mostly at the local level — making statewide coordination difficult.

Data centers are highly consumptive of the water they use. Google reports that 79% of the water used across its global data centers is consumed rather than returned, and Equinix reports a consumption rate of 76%. Water that isn't consumed still requires treatment through municipal wastewater systems before it can re-enter commercial or groundwater use.

Given these consumption rates, the most equitable and economically sound path forward is to build closer relationships between data centers and recycled water infrastructure — particularly when data centers invest in smaller municipalities that may lack the resources to expand recycled water access on their own. Santa Clara and San Jose, both major hubs for data center investment, already require the use of recycled water when it's available and offer discounted rates for it. As data center investment grows nationally and concentrates heavily in Silicon Valley, California is uniquely positioned to use this leverage to expand its economy while protecting its sustainability goals — and municipalities being courted by data center companies should use that leverage on behalf of their residents.

Effective incentives for co-investment in recycled water could include discounted recycled-water rates, tax breaks tied explicitly to infrastructure investment, and long-term contracts that favor recycled water over commercial or groundwater sources. Water Usage Efficiency (WUE) rebates would offer another practical lever to reduce consumption while supporting recycled water development.

Recycled water investment, however, is only part of the solution. A comprehensive system of reporting and review is equally necessary to prepare California's water systems for the demands data centers place on them.

Reporting Policy

Water sensitivity varies significantly across California. Some regions, including the Bay Area and parts of Los Angeles, are relatively well equipped to handle data center demand. Others, like Imperial County, would face severe strain given their existing reliance on imported water from the Colorado River.

A report by Next 10, The Intersection of Data Center Development, Water Availability, and Environmental Justice in California, found that this pattern of uneven impact repeats statewide — and that outdated legislation makes it easy for developers to avoid proper assessment altogether, leaving the true scale of impact largely unknown.

Under the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA), data centers can often be built without disclosing their community impact, using dated zoning processes such as ministerial approval and tiering to bypass environmental review. Assembly Bill 93, which would have required water-use reporting, was vetoed by Governor Gavin Newsom in 2025, leaving no clear path toward greater public transparency.

Opponents of mandated reporting often point to trade secrets and competitive pressure as reasons to resist disclosure. That argument doesn't hold up well against precedent: other industries already report environmental and public externalities, from greenhouse gas emissions at power plants to chemical disclosures at manufacturing facilities. Given how heavily data centers draw on shared water resources, the same standard should apply. Beyond the policy argument, fostering public distrust is also poor politics — much of the public skepticism toward data centers stems directly from a lack of transparency around water use, and reporting is the clearest path toward the public education needed to realize their economic benefits responsibly.

A revised bill, Assembly Bill 2619, currently represents the most promising path forward. It would mandate detailed water-consumption disclosures and require state agencies to develop best-practice guidelines by 2029, including standards for closed-loop cooling systems and non-potable water use. Santa Clara County — currently the only county in California where data centers disclose environmental information — demonstrates that this kind of transparency is both feasible and beneficial for communities and developers alike.

Reliable reporting would let water providers plan distribution ahead of time, help regulators identify which areas can sustainably support new data centers, and give auditors a basis for evaluating existing facilities. Most importantly, it would let the public properly weigh the costs and benefits of projects being built in their own communities.

Community Impact

Our research team visited the CoreSite campus in Santa Clara, one of the primary data center operators in the area, which also runs facilities in San Jose and Milpitas. CoreSite's buildings are unassuming — plain enough that most people would drive past without a second look. The Santa Clara campus houses seven separate data centers which, based on square footage and average industry water usage, likely consume up to 2 million gallons of water per day — roughly 746 million gallons per year by our estimate.

Those seven facilities represent only a fraction of the 83 data centers currently operating in Santa Clara and an estimated 270 statewide, with roughly 60 more under construction across California. As reservoirs shrink and heat intensifies the strain on already-finite water reserves, state leaders need to manage this growing sector proactively — using economic and social incentives to push data centers toward recycled water investment, while closing the data gap that currently limits effective oversight.

Conclusion

California has led technological advancement for decades, and it's in the state's interest to manage data center growth proactively rather than reactively. As construction accelerates nationwide and draws increasing public attention, California's legislature has an opportunity — and a responsibility — to respond to public concern with a clear framework for approval and environmental review.

Water security is the foremost concern surrounding data centers, and current reporting requirements and optimization efforts remain inadequate relative to the scale of the problem. We strongly support passage of Assembly Bill 2619 to close existing reporting loopholes and strengthen requirements around water recycling. It is only through measures like these that California can ensure residents share in the benefits of data center growth without bearing an outsized cost to shared water resources. California can continue to let data centers operate with limited transparency, straining public trust and fragile water systems — or it can lead the nation in building a regulatory framework that balances innovation with sustainability. The path forward is clear. It is time for the legislature to act.
